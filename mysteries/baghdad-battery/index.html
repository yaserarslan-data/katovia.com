Baghdad Battery

Did ancient Mesopotamians use electricity?

The case in 60 seconds

The crucial point about the Baghdad “Battery” is: being able to make it work as a battery does not establish that it was used as one in antiquity.

Small ceramic vessels found at Khujut Rabu near Baghdad in the 1930s contained a copper cylinder and iron rod. In 1938, Wilhelm König proposed that they might be galvanic cells.

Modern replicas have generated electricity with an acidic electrolyte. An experimental study published in 2026 even argued that a suitable arrangement could produce voltages above 1.4 volts and visible electrochemical effects.

But the archaeological problem remains: there are no electrical wires, series connections, definite electrolyte residues, instructions or indisputably electroplated objects. A substantial gap therefore remains between physical possibility and historical use.

The claim

“Ancient Mesopotamians knew electricity. They used the Baghdad Battery for gold plating and even medical electrotherapy.”

This statement treats three separate things as one piece of evidence: that the device can generate electricity, that people knew this, and that they used electricity for a specific purpose. An experiment can demonstrate the first. The second and third require archaeological context.

What we know

The combination of objects is realThe combination of ceramic jar, copper cylinder and iron rod was published in the 1930s.
Dating is uncertainThe find context is weak by modern standards, leaving uncertainty between Parthian and Sasanian dates.
A galvanic cell can be constructedCopper and iron, acting as different electrodes in an acidic liquid, can produce a small voltage.
“Electroplating” has not been directly establishedNo ancient Mesopotamian object is known to have been indisputably demonstrated as electrically plated.
Similar vessels offer alternativesComparable vessels from Seleucia and Ctesiphon have been compared with objects used to store magical/prayer texts.
The originals' fate is problematicThe original Baghdad finds were reported missing or of uncertain whereabouts after the 2003 looting; opportunities for reanalysis are severely limited.

The evidence

1 — Yes, it really can generate electricity

Copper and iron can act as two different metal electrodes. Vinegar, wine, grape juice or another acidic, ion-containing liquid between them can allow an electric current through a difference in chemical potential. This basic electrochemistry is not disputed.

Physically possible

2 — The 2026 experiment strengthened the hypothesis without resolving the history

Alexander Bazes's 2026 experimental study reported voltages above 1.4 volts and visible electrochemical reactions in a replica imitating the Baghdad Battery's details. It provides an important experimental result against the objection that “this design does not work”.

But the experiment shows how a modern researcher can operate the arrangement as a battery; it does not, by itself, show that an ancient user actually employed it for that purpose.

New and important; not evidence of intention

3 — The critical gap in the electroplating hypothesis

König's best-known proposal was electrically depositing gold. Archaeometallurgical specialists such as David A. Scott, however, emphasise the lack of direct evidence for electroplating in that region at that time. Thin gold coatings could be made using fire gilding, amalgam or other metallurgical techniques requiring no electricity.

The archaeological chain is missing

4 — If electricity was used, where is the circuit?

A functional technology involves more than a cell. Electroplating requires conductive connections, an object to coat, suitable electrolyte/metal salt and probably more than one cell. No reliable group of finds directly links these components with the Baghdad Battery.

One of the greatest difficulties

5 — The alternative “ritual/scroll container” explanation

Similar vessels at Ctesiphon have been interpreted as containers for magical, prayer or protective texts on papyrus. An organic text could have been placed inside the copper cylinder and completely decayed. Although the iron rod's function remains disputed in this model, a “ritual deposit” is a serious archaeological alternative.

A plausible alternative

6 — Is corrosion evidence of an electrolyte?

Corrosion on the iron and copper is sometimes described as residue from an acidic electrolyte. But buried metal objects also corrode through soil moisture and environmental chemistry. Without detailed re-examination of the original inner surfaces using modern analytical methods, this evidence cannot be considered decisive.

Uncertain

Theories

Theory A — A genuine galvanic battery / electroplating device

Strength: The geometry is suitable for an electric cell. Replicas generate electricity; the 2026 study also demonstrated stronger electrochemical effects.

Weakness: The use chain is absent: electrolyte residues, wires, series connections, a plating arrangement and definitely electroplated artefacts have not been found.

Theory B — Electrotherapy / pain relief

Strength: Paul Keyser proposed that the sensation of low-voltage electricity could have been used for pain relief or ritual-medical purposes.

Weakness: No ancient medical text describes this, and no electrode-application apparatus exists. Highly speculative.

Theory C — A container for magical / prayer / sacred texts

Strength: Similar regional vessels associated with written talismans and protective texts fit the historical context.

Weakness: A complete explanation of all details of the Khujut Rabu copper-and-iron arrangement is not straightforward.

Theory D — Another storage or craft vessel

Strength: An archaeological object's original use may not fit any modern classification.

Weakness: The need for the particular copper-cylinder/iron-rod combination must be explained.

My assessment

🟡 The Baghdad Battery could be a battery, but is not yet historically established as one.

Both extremes are problematic. Saying “ancient people could not use electricity, so it is impossible” is incorrect: the arrangement really can operate as an electrochemical cell.

The opposite conclusion—“it works, so it is definitely a battery”—is also incorrect. Understanding intention in archaeology requires functional context: wires, used electrolyte, an electroplated product, a series of cells or a text describing the process. None is definitely available.

In my view, the 2026 experiment made the case more interesting: it weakens the argument that “this could not have been a battery”. But it does not establish “ancient electrical technology”.

The most accurate statement at present is therefore: an ancient object strikingly suitable for generating electricity, whose original purpose remains unresolved.

Status in this series: 🟡 A genuine debate.
A battery function is physically possible. Archaeologically, it has not yet been shown to be the intended function.
