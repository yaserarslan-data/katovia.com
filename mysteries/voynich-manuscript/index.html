Voynich Manuscript

Which language or encoding system produced this text?

The conclusion in 60 seconds

The safest statement about the Voynich Manuscript is this: the book is real, medieval, and its text remains unreadable. But we also lack sufficient grounds to call it “entirely meaningless scribbling”.

The case's brief assessment: Voynich is a genuinely unsolved problem. But the mystery is not “which world did it come from?”; it is why people in the fifteenth century produced this book, and which encoding or writing system they used.

1. The claim

“What world's language does the Voynich Manuscript speak—a book containing strange plants and astronomical maps unlike anything on Earth, which no cryptologist or linguist has been able to decipher for centuries?”

Within this viral statement, four different questions are conflated:

1) Does the text actually carry meaning?   2) If it does, is it a natural language, a cipher, or an abbreviation system?   3) Do the drawings represent real objects?   4) What was the book's purpose?

“It has not been deciphered” is true. That does not establish that it is “non-human”, “the language of a lost civilisation” or “from another world”.

2. What we know

The Voynich Manuscript is an illustrated codex written on parchment, held at Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book & Manuscript Library as MS 408 . Yale's catalogue describes the text as an “unidentified writing system”; the illustrations are broadly divided into herbal, astronomical/astrological, balneological, cosmological, pharmaceutical and recipe-like sections.

Its historical trail — a short version

  • 1404–1438: The University of Arizona's radiocarbon analyses identify this interval as the most likely period in which the parchment was produced.
  • Fifteenth century: Analyses of the inks and pigments show that the materials are consistent with medieval manuscripts.
  • Seventeenth century: Johannes Marcus Marci's letter to Athanasius Kircher is an important link in the book's known early provenance; it recounts a second-hand story of a purchase involving Rudolf II.
  • 1912: Bookseller Wilfrid M. Voynich purchases the manuscript from a Jesuit collection in Italy; it becomes known in the modern world by his name.
  • 1969: H. P. Kraus donates it to Yale.
  • 2009: Material analyses and radiocarbon results substantially weaken the possibility of a modern forgery.
  • 2020: Lisa Fagin Davis argues for multiple scribal hands on the basis of palaeographic analysis; the most widely cited model proposes five distinct hands.
  • 2026: Layfield and Davis publish two studies examining textual coherence and binding structure; the second argues that the page order in which we read the book today may not be its original arrangement.

An important nuance: radiocarbon analysis measures the age of the parchment. It does not, by itself, establish the precise year in which the writing was added. However, ink/pigment analyses and historical style are broadly consistent with production in the early fifteenth century.

3. The evidence

A) Physical evidence: a genuine medieval object

Yale's conservation work and the McCrone Associates material analysis show that the book's parchment, inks and pigments are consistent with medieval manuscript production. In 2009, radiocarbon curves from four different parchment samples proved mutually consistent, and the combined interval was reported as 1404–1438.

What has been ruled out?

The material evidence makes the hypothesis that “Voynich is a modern forged book made in the nineteenth or twentieth century” very weak.

B) The text does not appear random

Two separate studies published in PLOS ONE in 2013 reached an important point of agreement. Amancio and colleagues reported that the Voynich text largely resembles natural languages on various network and word-statistical measures; Montemurro and Zanette reported topic-like clustering and information-bearing word networks in the long-range distribution of words.

These studies do not claim to have translated the text. Their finding is narrower but important: the distribution of symbol sequences across the pages is more regular than would be expected from simple random scribbling.

C) The text also differs strangely from natural languages

In 2022, Matlach, Janečková and Dostál found strong autocorrelations in the repetition behaviour of symbols that differed from the 21 natural languages and some encrypted examples they tested. “There is language-like structure” therefore does not mean “it behaves like one of the natural languages we know”.

This peculiarity leaves room for both a distinctive encoding system and rule-based artificial generation as hypotheses.

D) Sections and word use are related

There are measurable relationships between changes in word distribution and the visual sections conventionally labelled “herbal”, “astronomical” and so on. This reduces the likelihood that the drawings are merely decorations entirely independent of the text.

E) 2026: we may be reading the pages in the wrong order

A new development — 2026

Colin Layfield and Lisa Fagin Davis's peer-reviewed Digital Medievalist study considers binding structure alongside Latent Semantic Analysis results. It proposes that the manuscript may originally have been designed as a sequence of “singulions”—individually folded bifolia—rather than nested quires. If this is correct, the page order we see today may disrupt the original reading order.

This study does not decipher the text. But it changes something important: some “meaningless transitions” might be caused not by the language, but by a later rearrangement of the pages.

4. Five major questions

1) Is the text really a language?

The safest answer: it has statistical structure resembling natural language, but it has not been established that it must be a natural language written directly.

In 2021, Yale linguists Claire Bowern and Luke Lindemann reviewed the linguistic research and argued that the natural-language hypothesis should be taken seriously. Other researchers, however, show that Voynich-like structures can also be obtained through meaningless but rule-governed generation.

2) Is it a cipher?

It could be. But simple substitution ciphers of the familiar “letter A equals this symbol” kind have long appeared inadequate. Word positions within pages and lines, symbols occurring only in particular places, and word families may require a more complex mechanism: an abbreviation system, syllable-based code, multilayered cipher, steganographic structure, or a combination of these.

In 2022, Bowern and Gaskell showed that some word-level measures can remain consistent with certain forms of encryption. The inference “its statistics resemble language, so it cannot be a cipher” is therefore incorrect.

3) Could it be meaningless fabricated text?

Yes; that has not been entirely ruled out. In 2004, Gordon Rugg showed that Voynich-like words could be produced quickly using tables and methods resembling the Cardan grille known in the Renaissance. This refutes the claim that “such a hoax was technically impossible in the fifteenth century”.

The difficulty, however, is that an explanation must account not only for the appearance of the words, but also for long-range distributions, differences between sections, repetition patterns and possible behaviour of multiple scribes. A hoax is possible, but it has not been “easily proven”.

4) Do the plant drawings depict real species?

According to the cautious descriptions in Yale and PLOS sources, most plants cannot be unambiguously matched to a single real species. Several ordinary explanations are possible: stylisation within the medieval herbal tradition, combinations of parts from different plants, distortion through successive copying, or symbolic/functional drawing.

“Plants unlike anything on Earth” is a visually appealing statement, but scientifically weak. Failure to identify a plant does not mean it does not exist in nature.

5) What do the astronomical/astrological diagrams show?

Some motifs, such as zodiac signs and star shapes, can clearly be related to the medieval astrological tradition. Nevertheless, it is unknown whether the diagrams specifically represent a calendar, astrological calculations, medical astrology, cosmology, or some other system.

The problem here is the same: we recognise the type of image, but cannot read its caption. Until the text is deciphered, we must settle for strong but uncertain interpretations of the diagrams' purpose.

5. Theories

There is no “single grand theory” of Voynich. A better approach is to examine which data several competing models explain well or poorly.

🗣️ Theory 1 — A natural language written in an unknown script

Strength: word frequencies, topic-related distributions and some long-range relationships resemble natural texts. Weakness: some constraints on symbol and word structures are unusually rigid for known languages; there is no reliable match to a language.

🔐 Theory 2 — An encrypted/abbreviated form of a known language

Strength: ciphers, abbreviations and shorthand systems were used in the medieval and early modern periods; these might explain Voynich's unusual symbol distribution. Weakness: after more than a century of analysis, there is no key that works throughout the book and whose results independent researchers can reproduce.

🧠 Theory 3 — An artificial/constructed language

Strength: it could explain rules resembling natural languages but fitting no language family. Prominent cryptologists such as William Friedman historically took an interest in this possibility. Weakness: there is no direct evidence of the language's vocabulary or grammar; “artificial language” sometimes merely gives the unknown a new name.

🎭 Theory 4 — A deliberate hoax / rule-governed text without meaning

Strength: Rugg and later generative models show that people can produce rule-governed but meaningless Voynich-like text. Weakness: it is difficult to explain the book's organisation at section level, relationships between drawings and text, and subtle statistical patterns within the same generative model.

📚 Theory 5 — A medical/herbal/astrological reference book

Strength: plants, containers, body/bathing scenes, the zodiac and stars are thematically consistent with medieval medical, herbal and astrological literature. Weakness: because the text cannot be read, more specific descriptions such as “a women's health manual” or “pharmaceutical recipes” cannot be directly verified.

6. Social media / internet claims

❌ “It depicts plants unlike anything on Earth.”

An overinterpretation. Most drawings cannot be reliably identified; this does not mean they have no real-world counterpart. Stylisation and composite drawings are possible within the medieval herbal tradition.

❌ “No linguist or cryptologist has learned anything about the text.”

False. There is no translation, but we know a great deal that can be measured about word statistics, symbol positions, Currier A/B differences, section clustering and scribal hands.

❌ “AI has deciphered Voynich.”

Not verified. Computer models can suggest candidate languages and structural patterns, but no independently reproducible translation working across all pages has been produced.

❌ “It was established as Proto-Romance in 2019.”

No. Gerard Cheshire's paper was published, but received serious methodological criticism from linguists and medieval specialists. The University of Bristol withdrew its initial publicity story following these criticisms. The proposal did not become an academic consensus.

🟡 “It has been deciphered as Old Turkish.”

This is not an accepted solution either. Turkish/Turkic language hypotheses attracted public interest, but no academic consensus has emerged around a translation of the whole text using the same transparent, independently applicable method.

🟡 “It is definitely a women's health book.”

That could be one of its themes; naked female figures, baths and plants encourage this interpretation. But because the text cannot be read, presenting this as “definite content” exceeds the limits of the data.

7. What remains unknown

❓ Language / codeThe language, encoding method or generative system underlying the text is unknown.
❓ Authors and locationWe do not know who produced the book, precisely where, or within which institution or circle.
❓ PurposeA medical book, a private reference notebook, teaching material, an esoteric work—it is not certain.
❓ Meaning of the drawingsWhat many plants, pipes, pools and cosmological shapes represent is unclear.
❓ Original page orderIf the 2026 “singulion” hypothesis is correct, the present binding arrangement may have disrupted the original flow of the text.
❓ Why are there no other examples?If a community really used this writing system, it is unknown why no other definite examples have survived.

The real mystery: This is not a book about which we know nothing. On the contrary, there is a surprising amount of data about its physical history and textual structure. What is missing is the key that brings all these data together into one testable, readable explanation.

8. My assessment

In my view, the most important message of the evidence is this: presenting Voynich as an “alien language” inflates the mystery while diminishing the scientific question. The real problem is much more interesting: why does the writing system of a book physically consistent with fifteenth-century Europe resist modern linguistics and cryptology so persistently?

Does the text carry meaning?

The balance of evidence points towards a system that is rule-governed and probably information-bearing, rather than simple random scribbling. But “statistically language-like” is not the same as “proven to be a natural language”.

Which category is most plausible?

A natural language with unusual spelling/abbreviation, or some family of encoding, seems to me a better starting hypothesis than purely random fabrication. This is not a conclusion, however: there is still no general key that works on the text.

Why does the 2026 finding matter?

If the original page order has been disrupted, some earlier linguistic models may have been built on incorrect adjacencies. This does not mean “a solution has been found”, but it could bring a real methodological change to future research.

My minimum test for taking a proposed Voynich solution seriously would be: the same rules must yield consistent text on previously unused pages, not merely on a few selected words; other researchers must reach the same result using the same key. So far, no popular “I've solved it” claim has passed this threshold in a way that has gained broad acceptance.

9. Status

🔴 GENUINELY UNSOLVED — but we are not entirely in the dark

Largely answered: That the object is a genuine medieval manuscript.

Strong evidence: That the text is more regular than a simple random sequence of symbols and is structured at section level.

Strong possibility: That the text carries linguistic or encoded information.

Still disputed: Which explanation is correct: natural language, cipher, abbreviation system, artificial language, or rule-governed fabricated text.

Unsolved: A reliable, reproducible translation that works throughout the book.

Unsupported: Definitive viral claims such as “a language from another world”, “AI has definitely solved it”, or “it has now been proven to be this language”.
